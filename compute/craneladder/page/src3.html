From So Simple a Beginning

An experiment in where design comes from. Random 64-byte programs, ten instructions, no fitness function and no replicator written in. Stage 1 asked whether copiers arise on their own. In one of four runs, they did, and this page shows how.

“…from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.” Darwin, 1859

1 · THE ARGUMENT

Cranes, skyhooks, and who pays for the information

Dembski and Marks put the argument in numbers. Any search that hits a target more often than blind sampling has been given information, and they measure how much in bits: active information is log₂ of the search’s success rate divided by blind sampling’s. Their conservation theorem adds that a search this good is at least as hard to find, by blind search among searches, as the target was to begin with. Evolution, on this view, can only spend information that was put in somewhere.

This project builds rungs from random programs and keeps books on each one. Nothing scores the programs and no copier is written in, so if copiers appear, the dynamics found them. The books record three numbers per rung, all in bits:

Chance cost

How improbable the target is under blind sampling. For Stage 1’s main target, a soup whose programs are full of copies, it is at least

8,388,608 bits

One bit per byte of the soup: 131,072 programs × 64 bytes. By counting, no more than one random soup in 28,388,608 compresses this well.

Active information

How much the rules raise the odds over blind sampling. One run in four reached the target, so it is at least about

8,388,606 bits

Chance cost minus log₂(4). The figure grows with the size of the soup; the comparison that matters is with the red column.

Designer bits

Everything put in by hand: the rule code, every configuration tried, every labelled shortcut. So far:

≤ 3,184 bits, plus the rarity of the rules

3,184 bits is cubff’s rule code compressed. One configuration was tried and nothing was injected. How rare rules like these are among all simple rule sets is what Stage 2 measures, and it is where Dembski’s accounting would have to find the other 8.4 million bits.

2 · THE LADDER

Four rungs and the ground they stand on

Each rung is a stage of the project, read from the ground up. A crane means the rung has been reached from the one below with nothing injected; a dashed cable means it is not reached yet. Every stage runs a sham beside the real thing and waits for a go-ahead before it starts.

3 · THE RULES

What the programs are, and what happens to them

Nothing scores a program and nothing is removed. About one byte in 4,096 is replaced at random in each pairing, and that is the only outside change. A program spreads only if running it writes a copy of itself over its partner. Of the 256 possible byte values, ten are instructions and the rest do nothing.

The rules are BFF, from Agüera y Arcas and colleagues (2024), run with their own code, cubff, at their size and settings.

4 · THE TAKEOVER

Seed 2: two copier families, one poisoned by its own zeros

Each dot is one of 2,000 programs at fixed places in the soup, followed through time. Programs with similar code sit near each other: the code of each is turned into a list of which instruction sits at which byte, and the 640-number lists are squashed into three dimensions. Random programs make a loose cloud. Copies of one program sit on top of each other, so a family that copies itself pulls its dots into clumps. Drag to turn it.

5 · THE COPIER

How the winner copies: a mirror, twice

The ring is the 128-byte tape of one pairing, bent into a circle: the copier on the right, its partner on the left, and the tape’s first byte at the top. Head 1 reads down the copier; head 0 writes down the partner. Every copy joins a byte to its mirror image across the ring, so the partner ends up holding the copier back to front.
